Executive summary — read this first

The gap: Blinkit's monthly customers doubled in FY26 (13.7M → 27.2M) while net AOV stayed flat at ₹525 — growth is repeat baskets, not new categories, and profit sits at 0.3% of order value vs a 5–6% target.

The evidence: two independent methods agree. 29 source-linked public records (complaint boards, review platforms, regulatory reporting, four market-research houses) and a verified survey of 14 quick-commerce users across 8 cities both point to the same blocker: documented trust failure, not lack of appetite. Beauty & personal care proves the upside — it grew from 2.8% to 8.5% of quick-commerce GMV once trust formed.

The surprise: desk research predicted expiry/freshness proof was the unlock. Users disagreed — 11 of 14 chose real customer ratings & photos. The product was rebuilt around their answer.

The outcome: all three hypotheses validated (H1 11/14, H2 12/14, H3 10/14). Aisle Buddy — one peer-proofed, trial-size pick per basket — is live and measures itself on a shown → added → purchased funnel.

How to use this repository: Section 1 is the proof, Section 2 the method, Section 3 the fieldwork kit, Section 4 is the primary-survey findings that reshaped the product.

Section 2 — Discovery-engine methodology & quality gate

Pipeline (live in the Voice Miner app):

  • Gather — seed corpus hand-collected from public sources (above). Scale path: Apify scrapers for Play Store / App Store / Reddit on an n8n cron.
  • Clean — paraphrase, de-duplicate, strip PII; one record per voice with source metadata.
  • Tag — a classifier assigns each record one of six themes. The same classifier runs live in the app.
  • Dashboard — theme counts computed from records; updates live as new reviews are classified.
  • Ask — grounded evidence search: research questions are answered ONLY from the corpus, citing record IDs.
Quality gate. Every seed record links to a verifiable public source; tags are human-reviewable in-app; the classifier forces one theme per record and a neutral paraphrase. Known bias, handled. Complaint boards over-represent anger, so every negative signal was triangulated two ways: against independent market data (Redseer, Mordor, Bain, Eternal) and against a separate primary survey with its own recruitment. Both converged on trust. Counter-evidence is reported in Section 4.5, and sample limits in Section 4.6.

Section 3 — User interview kit

Research status: complete. Phase 1 — desk research across complaint forums, review platforms, regulatory reporting and four market-research houses (n=29). Phase 2 — a verified primary survey of 14 quick-commerce users across 8 Indian cities, run August 2026 with recorded consent. Both phases are done; findings are in Section 4, and they changed the product (see 4.4).

3.1 Screener (recruit 5–6 respondents)

Target segment: “Routine Replenishers”

  • Age 24–40, lives in a metro (Bengaluru / NCR / Mumbai / Hyderabad / Chennai)
  • Orders on Blinkit/Zepto/Instamart 6+ times a month (verify from order history)
  • Self-reports that most orders repeat the same items (groceries / snacks / household)
  • Screen out: works in q-commerce/e-commerce; bought 3+ non-food categories last month (already an explorer)

3.2 Consent script (read aloud, record verbal yes)

“Thanks for making time. This is a 30–45 minute conversation for a product research project on quick-commerce shopping habits. I'll take notes and may record audio only for my own note-taking. Nothing will be shared with your name attached, and you can skip any question or stop at any time. Are you comfortable proceeding?”

3.3 Discussion guide (semi-structured, 40 min)

  • Warm-up (5 min) — Walk me through the last time you opened Blinkit. What did you order? What triggered it?
  • Habit & mission (10 min) — How do you decide what to buy — list in your head, search, or browse? How long does a typical session take? What do you NEVER buy on quick commerce, and why not?
  • Trust & risk (10 min) — Tell me about a time a quick-commerce order disappointed you. What happened next? Did it change what you buy? If you needed a face wash / pet food / a gift tonight, where would you get it — and why not Blinkit?
  • Discovery & triggers (10 min) — When did you last try something new on any app — what convinced you? What would a platform have to show you before you'd risk a category you've never bought there?
  • Wrap (5 min) — Magic wand: one change that would make you shop more categories here?

Do not mention Aisle Buddy or any solution until the wrap.

3.4 Hypotheses under test (interviewer keeps hidden)

  • H1 (MISSION): Sessions are list-driven missions with near-zero browse time.
  • H2 (TRUST): Unfamiliar categories are avoided due to expiry/quality/return risk, not lack of interest. (Strongest corpus signal: 16 of 19 user-voice records.)
  • H3 (PRICE): Fees and price checks suppress add-on items at checkout.
  • Open question: what proof (freshness info, ratings, refunds, trial sizes) would unlock a first trial?

3.5 Note template (one per respondent)

Respondent code (P1..P6) · date · city · orders/month · top categories · Trigger for last order · Session behaviour (search vs browse, duration) · Categories avoided + stated reason (verbatim) · Disappointment story + behavioural consequence · Where they buy beauty/pet/baby/gifts today · Proof that would unlock a first trial (verbatim) · H1/H2/H3 support (Y/N/partial + evidence) · New insight

Section 4 — Findings from the primary survey (n=14)

Fielded August 2026 via a verified Google Form (one response per verified email, explicit consent). 14 complete responses from Bengaluru, Chennai, Hyderabad, Coimbatore, Noida, Vadodara, Kurnool and Tellapur. Ages: 10 respondents 18–24, 3 aged 25–34, 1 aged 35–44. Order frequency: 5 place 3–5 orders a month, 4 place 6–10, 1 places more than 10, 4 place 1–2.

4.1 Hypothesis verdicts

HypothesisSupportVerdictWhat the data showed
H1 — Mission-mode habit
Sessions are need-driven, not browsing trips		11/14VALIDATED“Ran out of something” was the single biggest trigger (8/14) and 9/14 shop by searching exact items or working from a list. Only 1 respondent opened the app just to browse.
H2 — Trust deficit
New categories are avoided over risk, not disinterest		12/14VALIDATED — strongest9 of 14 reported a real bad experience. Avoidance reasons are risk-shaped: returns feel impossible (4), prefer to see and touch first (4), don’t trust freshness (3). Only 3 said a category simply never occurred to them.
H3 — Price & fee anxiety
Fees and price checks suppress add-ons		10/14VALIDATED — upgraded9 of 14 check the bill closely and sometimes drop items before paying; 4 name delivery fees as a reason to avoid a category outright. Desk research had ranked price a distant third — the survey moved it up.

4.2 The finding that changed the product

Desk research predicted the wrong fix. The 29-record corpus pointed at expiry and freshness proof as the unlock. Real users disagreed.

Asked what an app must show before they would try a new category, 11 of 14 chose “real customer ratings & photos” — ahead of clear expiry/freshness info (7), small trial packs under ₹250 (5), and easy returns (4). Only one respondent said nothing would convince them.

Why it makes sense: 4 respondents said they avoid categories because they “prefer to see and touch things first.” Photos and peer ratings are the closest digital substitute for picking an item up in a shop — something an expiry date alone cannot provide.

What changed: Aisle Buddy’s suggestion card now leads with peer proof — a visible rating, review count and buyer photo count — with freshness/returns framing kept as support. The evidence reordered the product, not the other way round.

4.3 What people actually buy — and avoid

Categories already tried at least once: gourmet snacks & beverages (7), beauty/skincare (6), electronics & accessories (6), kitchen & home tools (6), gifts (2), pet supplies (2). Three respondents had never bought outside groceries at all.

Most-avoided: electronics & accessories (5) — notable because market data names it the fastest-growing quick-commerce category, so the appetite gap here is trust, not demand. Baby care, pet supplies and beauty follow at 3 each. Encouragingly, 4 of 14 said they would “try anything” — latent openness the current app does nothing to convert.

  • P1 — Yes, Wrong item

  • P3 — I once received vegetables with an insect inside, which was disappointing. Since then, I don't fully trust the freshness of fruits and vegetables from quick-commerce apps & only order them when necessary
  • P6 — Most of the time, I got melted ice cream. Even when I tried to return or get a refund, customer service was often busy and would say they'd get back to me, which they never did, so I'd just give up.
  • P7 — Yeah sometimes it disappointed with wrong items
  • P10 — Quality, wrong iteam, refund trouble
  • P11 — The fungus bread
  • P12 — I ordered few medicine using net meds, while I was travelling. I got the expired and damaged product.
  • P14 — Yes so many times

Note the pattern: the damage is rarely the product alone — it is the unresolved aftermath. P2 and P6 both describe failed refund attempts, and both ended in the customer giving up. This mirrors the desk corpus exactly (16 of 19 user-voice records concern trust), so two independent methods converged on the same conclusion.

4.5 Counter-evidence, kept honest

Not every respondent was unhappy. P8 reported no problems at all and rated their experience 10/10, and 5 of 14 had no bad incident to report. Two respondents wanted breadth rather than trust — more variety and fewer out-of-stock items (P8), and clothing as a category (P14). A trust-first product must not assume every customer is wary; that is why Aisle Buddy’s suggestion is capped at one per basket and always dismissible.

4.6 Limitations

n=14 is directional, not statistically representative; the sample skews young (10 of 14 aged 18–24) and South-Indian, and was recruited through the researcher’s network, so it carries referral bias. Findings are strongest where they converge with the independent 29-record desk corpus — as H2 does. A larger, age-balanced wave and behavioural A/B data are the natural next steps before company-wide decisions.
